The smallest architecture is not “a secure agent.” It is a continuity of authority that survives the replacement of every component that happens to implement it.

The core distinction is this:

The sovereign domain is a logical actor whose authority is defined by governed state transitions, not by possession of data, credentials, compute, identity artifacts, or infrastructure.

Everything else follows from that.

1. The irreducible state

A sovereign domain needs one logically authoritative state, even if that state is physically replicated, sharded, encoded, backed up, or reconstructed.

Call it Sovereign State \(S\).

At minimum, \(S\) must determine:

$$ S = ( I,\, A,\, R,\, D,\, G,\, H,\, C ) $$

where:

\(I\): actor identity and continuity rules
\(A\): currently valid authorities
\(R\): rights, obligations, relationships and constraints
\(D\): delegations currently in force
\(G\): governance and recovery rules
\(H\): authoritative transition history or commitments sufficient to reconstruct it
\(C\): current constitutional/version state, including what rules determine valid future transitions

This does not mean those fields require a particular database, ledger, key system or serialization. It means that, whatever implementation exists, there must be an unambiguous answer to:

“What state currently determines what this actor is allowed to do, who may act for it, and how that state may legitimately change?”

If there is no such answer, sovereignty has already been lost.

2. The minimal invariants

I think only nine fundamental invariants are required. Most other desirable properties reduce to them.

Invariant 1 — Actor continuity is independent of replaceable artifacts

No replaceable artifact may be identical with the sovereign actor.

Therefore:

$$ Actor \neq Device \neq Key \neq Credential \neq Account \neq Runtime \neq Model \neq Provider $$

A replacement operation changes an implementation binding, not the actor.

Consequently there must exist rules for deciding that:

$$ Actor_{before} = Actor_{after} $$

despite changes in keys, devices, providers, software or infrastructure.

Without this invariant, key rotation, device migration or provider migration becomes identity replacement.

Invariant 2 — Authority comes only from authoritative state

An operation has authority only because the current sovereign state says that it does.

Not because:

a service possesses data,
a user successfully authenticated,
a model generated the operation,
a credential verifies,
a message arrived through a trusted channel,
a provider hosts the state,
a device was previously trusted,
a worker was assigned the task,
an agent possesses a capability,
or an operation was authorized earlier.

Formally, for consequential operation \(x\):

$$ Authorized(x,t) \iff ValidUnder(x,S_t,E_t) $$

where \(S_t\) is authoritative state at the relevant decision point and \(E_t\) represents required external conditions.

Everything else is merely evidence supplied to that decision.

This is perhaps the most important invariant in the system.

Invariant 3 — Every consequential transition is explicitly authorized

Compute can happen freely within its allowed information boundary.

Authority cannot happen implicitly.

Any transition that alters the sovereign domain or causes an external consequence must pass through an authority boundary.

So:

$$ Proposal \not\Rightarrow Consequence $$

Instead:

$$ Proposal \rightarrow Authorization \rightarrow Commitment \rightarrow Execution $$

These may sometimes collapse into a single atomic operation, but they remain logically distinct.

The fact that a system can perform an action must never imply that it may perform that action.

Invariant 4 — Authorization is evaluated against current state

Authorization cannot merely mean “someone approved this once.”

Between proposal and consequence:

authority may disappear,
delegation may expire,
a relationship may terminate,
scope may change,
purpose may change,
the underlying object may change,
external conditions may change,
approval thresholds may change,
revocation may occur.

Therefore a consequential action must be bound to a sufficiently precise state/context and either:

execute within an indivisible authorization/commitment transition; or
be revalidated before consequence.

Conceptually:

$$ Authorize(P,S_a) $$

does not establish:

$$ Execute(P,S_b) $$

unless the authorization explicitly remains valid under the transition from \(S_a\) to \(S_b\).

This eliminates stale authority.

Invariant 5 — Delegation is bounded, attributable and revocable

Delegated authority must never become indistinguishable from original authority.

Every delegation must answer at least:

who delegated,
to whom,
what authority,
over what objects/domain,
for what purpose,
under what constraints,
for how long / until what condition,
whether/how it may be redelegated,
how it is revoked.

Thus:

$$ DelegatedAuthority \subseteq DelegatorAuthority $$

A delegate cannot manufacture authority the delegator did not possess.

Delegation can be narrower but cannot silently become broader.

Invariant 6 — Information access and authority are orthogonal

Possessing information is not possession of authority.

A component may have:

all the data and no authority,
authority to perform one operation and almost no data,
neither,
or narrowly scoped quantities of both.

Therefore the system must conceptually separate:

$$ DataAccess $$

from:

$$ DecisionAuthority $$

from:

$$ ExecutionAuthority $$

This is what permits external models, clouds and workers to be useful without becoming sovereign.

Invariant 7 — External disclosure is purpose-bounded

An external compute system receives only information legitimately required for its assigned purpose.

That implies a transformation:

$$ S \xrightarrow{\text{derive for purpose } p} V_p $$

where \(V_p\) is a view, not sovereign state itself.

The recipient operates on \(V_p\).

It does not acquire authority over \(S\).

A useful consequence follows:

The normal unit of interaction with external intelligence should be a purpose-specific derived view, not unrestricted access to the sovereign domain.

No particular privacy mechanism is implied. It is the boundary that matters.

Invariant 8 — Evidence and authoritative state are different things

Evidence can establish that something occurred without making the contents authoritative.

For example, it may prove:

model M received context X,
worker W proposed action P,
authority service A approved P,
executor E submitted request Q,
provider returned result R.

None of these facts imply that:

X was true,
P was correct,
R was legitimate,
the model had authority,
or the sovereign state actually changed.

Therefore:

$$ Evidence(event) \neq Authority(event) $$

and:

$$ Evidence(content) \neq Truth(content) $$

Only a valid state transition changes authoritative state.

This prevents logs, transcripts, AI outputs, signed statements and provider records from silently becoming constitutional truth.

Invariant 9 — Constitutional control has a recoverable root

Every replaceable mechanism may disappear.

Therefore the rules by which sovereign authority can be recovered cannot ultimately depend upon any one such mechanism.

Recovery must be able to establish:

which actor is being recovered,
which authoritative state or valid successor is recovered,
which prior authorities are preserved or revoked,
which implementation bindings are replaced,
why the recovery transition itself is legitimate.

This yields:

$$ Loss(Component) \not\Rightarrow Loss(Sovereignty) $$

although it may cause:

$$ Loss(Component) \Rightarrow Loss(Capability) $$

Those are deliberately different outcomes.

A sovereign system can tolerate incapacity.

It cannot tolerate involuntary transfer of constitutional control.

3. The necessary boundaries

From those invariants, only a few logical boundaries are required.

Boundary A — Sovereign state boundary

Inside lies authoritative state.

Outside lies everything that may observe, cache, process, reproduce or propose changes to it.

Only valid transition machinery may cross this boundary in the mutating direction.

Conceptually:

                observations
                     ↓
        ┌────────────────────────┐
        │    Sovereign State     │
        │                        │
        │ identity               │
        │ authority              │
        │ rights                 │
        │ delegation             │
        │ governance             │
        │ recovery               │
        └───────────┬────────────┘
                    │
            valid transition
                    │
                    ▼
                  S'

No database or machine is inherently “inside.” The boundary is logical.

Boundary B — Intelligence boundary

Models, algorithms, analysts, agents, simulations and optimizers sit on the non-authoritative side.

They can produce:

$$ Observation \rightarrow Analysis \rightarrow Proposal $$

but never:

$$ Proposal \equiv Authority $$

An AI may be extraordinarily capable while remaining constitutionally powerless.

That is precisely the desired property.

Boundary C — Authorization boundary

This resolves:

“May this proposed transition occur now?”

Inputs may include:

current authoritative state,
proposal,
provenance,
delegation,
purpose,
constraints,
required approvals,
environmental facts,
freshness,
revocation state.

Its output is not merely “yes/no.”

It must bind approval to what was actually approved.

For example:

$$ Auth = f( Actor, Operation, Target, Parameters, Purpose, StateReference, Constraints, Validity ) $$

Otherwise an approval for one thing can be replayed or transformed into approval for another.

Boundary D — Consequence boundary

A separate distinction exists between changing internal authoritative state and causing effects elsewhere.

Examples include:

transferring money,
opening a lock,
publishing a statement,
entering a contract,
commanding machinery,
issuing an access grant.

At this boundary the system must ensure the attempted external consequence still corresponds to valid authority.

This is where stale authorization, substitution and confused-deputy failures must be prevented.

Boundary E — Evidence boundary

Evidence enters an append/preserve process but does not enter authoritative state merely because it exists.

This gives two separate streams:

                   ┌──> Evidence history
Event / Result ────┤
                   │
                   └──> Proposed state transition
                             │
                          validation
                             │
                             ▼
                     Authoritative state

This distinction is essential to reconstructibility.

4. The minimum state-transition model

The entire world can be described with surprisingly few transition types.

1. Observe

Acquire information.

$$ S \rightarrow (S,O) $$

No authority changes.

Observation may later become evidence.

2. Derive

Produce a purpose-limited view.

$$ (S,p) \rightarrow V_p $$

No authoritative state changes.

This is how sovereign information leaves the boundary.

3. Propose

An agent, model, person or process proposes a change.

$$ (S,V,O) \rightarrow P $$

Still no authoritative change.

4. Authorize

Current sovereign state evaluates whether P may proceed.

$$ (S,P,E) \rightarrow A $$

where \(A\) is a scoped authorization.

Still not necessarily a state change or external consequence.

5. Commit

A valid authorization becomes an authoritative transition:

$$ (S,A) \rightarrow S' $$

This is the only operation that changes sovereign truth.

Examples include:

grant delegation,
revoke delegation,
accept relationship,
change policy,
rotate implementation binding,
record an authoritative decision.
6. Execute

A currently valid authorization is presented at the consequence boundary:

$$ (A,S,E) \rightarrow X $$

where \(X\) is an attempted external consequence.

The result may succeed, fail or be ambiguous.

Critically:

$$ Attempt \neq Success $$

and:

$$ Success \neq AuthorizedStateChange $$

unless sovereign rules explicitly connect them.

7. Record evidence

Every meaningful stage may generate durable evidence:

$$ event \rightarrow e $$

Evidence can cover:

observed
derived
disclosed
proposed
authorized
denied
committed
attempted
returned
failed
revoked
recovered

Evidence remains evidence.

8. Revoke

Authority is changed so that some previously available transition is no longer valid.

$$ S \rightarrow S' $$

The important semantic requirement is that subsequent authorization/execution observes the revocation.

It is impossible in a general distributed world to guarantee that every previously leaked datum disappears. Sovereignty therefore has to depend on revocation of authority, not magical revocation of knowledge.

9. Replace

Change an implementation artifact.

$$ (S,B_x) \rightarrow (S,B_y) $$

where \(B\) means an implementation binding.

Notice:

$$ S_{identity}=S_{identity} $$

The actor did not change.

This operation covers:

key rotation,
new phone,
new cloud,
new model,
new runtime,
new storage system,
new executor,
new credential.
10. Recover

Restore operative control from survivable authoritative information and governance.

$$ RecoveryEvidence + Governance \rightarrow S' $$

Recovery is itself an authoritative transition and must obey constitutional rules.

Otherwise “recovery” is merely a privileged takeover path.

5. The minimal lifecycle for consequential action

The safe primitive is therefore:

PURPOSE
   │
   ▼
DERIVE MINIMUM VIEW
   │
   ▼
COMPUTE / REASON
   │
   ▼
PROPOSAL
   │
   ▼
VALIDATE AGAINST CURRENT SOVEREIGN STATE
   │
   ▼
AUTHORIZE
   │
   ├──── state changed / authorization stale ──> reject or reauthorize
   │
   ▼
COMMIT / EXECUTE
   │
   ▼
OBSERVE RESULT
   │
   ▼
EVIDENCE
   │
   └──── if authoritative update required ─────> new validated transition

That loop is the essential machine.

Everything else is an optimization.

6. A particularly important rule: never confuse nouns with powers

Many architectures fail because they attach sovereignty to a noun.

For example:

“The key is the identity.”

That fails when the key must be replaced.

“The phone is the trusted device.”

That fails when the phone disappears.

“The account owns the data.”

That fails when the provider disappears.

“The AI agent is acting for the user.”

That fails when the model is compromised or replaced.

“The signed request is authorized.”

That fails when authority has since been revoked.

“The ledger says it happened.”

That fails when recorded evidence is mistaken for authoritative truth.

The correct formulation is always relational:

Under current sovereign state, this evidence establishes that this principal currently has this authority, for this purpose, over this scope, subject to these constraints.

That relation can survive replacement of the nouns involved.

7. Four distinct concepts that should never collapse

A minimal implementation must preserve four different kinds of state.

Kind	Means	Example
Authoritative state	What the sovereign domain currently declares operative	Alice may spend ≤ $500
Proposal	What some party suggests should happen	Transfer $300
Authorization	Permission for a precisely bounded consequence	This transfer may execute under conditions X
Evidence	Durable information about events	Agent proposed it; authority approved it; bank returned success

Collapsing any two creates severe problems.

Proposal = authorization

The model becomes sovereign.

Authentication = authorization

Anyone holding an authentication mechanism acquires unintended powers.

Evidence = state

Logs become constitutional truth.

Execution result = state

External services determine sovereign truth.

Credential = identity

Credential replacement becomes actor replacement.

Hosted state = authority

The storage provider becomes sovereign.

Those equivalences therefore have to be prohibited at the architectural level.

8. The reconstruction invariant

“Backup” is weaker than what your conditions demand.

The system needs semantic reconstructibility.

After catastrophic replacement, a valid recovery process must be able to determine at least:

Who is the actor?

What constitutional rules govern it?

What is the latest defensible authoritative state?

How was that state reached?

Which delegations remain valid?

Which have expired or been revoked?

Which relationships and obligations remain?

Which implementation bindings are obsolete?

What unresolved external actions may still be in flight?

What evidence exists regarding them?

What recovery authority permits the new implementation to continue?

This does not require reproducing every bit that ever existed.

It requires preserving enough information to reconstruct the operative sovereign domain.

That means the survival target is not:

$$ all\ data $$

but:

$$ constitution + authoritative\ state + transition\ lineage + recovery\ basis + unresolved\ commitments $$

Everything else may be recoverable, reproducible or expendable according to policy.

9. The subtle distributed-systems consequence

There is one unavoidable limitation.

The world contains components that can disappear during actions.

Therefore some outcomes will be uncertain.

For example:

authorize payment
→ send payment
→ network fails
→ did the bank receive it?

No architecture can universally infer the answer from silence.

So sovereign reconstruction must admit an explicit state such as:

$$ Outcome = Unknown $$

rather than inventing false certainty.

Consequently transitions involving external systems need stable operation identity so that reconciliation can distinguish:

never attempted,
attempted,
definitely failed,
definitely succeeded,
outcome unknown,
subsequently reconciled.

Thus uncertainty itself must be representable authoritative state where external atomicity is unavailable.

That is necessary, not optional.

10. Replacement without sovereignty transfer

A component migration should therefore conceptually look like:

Old implementation
       │
       │  proves / exports sufficient state and evidence
       ▼
Sovereign continuity process
       │
       │  governed replacement transition
       ▼
New implementation

and never:

New provider says:
"I authenticated successfully,
therefore I am now the sovereign root."

The replacement is legitimate because the sovereign domain authorized a new binding.

The new binding does not legitimize itself.

This inversion is critical.

11. What must be durable?

Not everything.

The least-resistance architecture makes only a small constitutional core durable.

Necessarily durable

Enough to recover:

actor continuity,
current authoritative state,
governance rules,
delegation state,
revocations,
rights/relationships/obligations that survive replacement,
transition lineage sufficient to establish the legitimacy of current state,
unresolved consequential operations,
recovery rules.
Desirably durable

Evidence required for:

audit,
dispute resolution,
accountability,
provenance,
reconstruction,
reconciliation.
Not constitutionally necessary

Unless application policy says otherwise:

model scratchpads,
temporary caches,
intermediate computation,
runtime state,
provider-specific metadata,
replicated derived views,
ephemeral session state.

This separation substantially simplifies the architecture.

12. The smallest possible conceptual architecture

You can reduce the entire system to five logical functions.

                    ┌─────────────────────┐
                    │  Sovereign State    │
                    │ + Constitution      │
                    └──────────┬──────────┘
                               │
                 ┌─────────────┴─────────────┐
                 │                           │
                 ▼                           ▼
          VIEW DERIVATION              AUTHORITY
       minimum necessary data       validate transitions
                 │                           │
                 ▼                           │
       EXTERNAL COMPUTATION                  │
       models / agents / clouds              │
                 │                           │
                 └────── proposal ───────────┘
                                             │
                                             ▼
                                      CONSEQUENCE
                                        execution
                                             │
                                             ▼
                                         EVIDENCE

Plus one cross-cutting operation:

RECOVERY / REPLACEMENT

which can reconstruct or rebind every box without changing the logical actor.

No more fundamental architecture is required.

Individual implementations may split these into hundreds of services or collapse them into one device.

The logical invariants remain identical.

13. The constitutional kernel

If reduced even further, the sovereign domain needs to be able to answer exactly six questions:

Who am I?
What is currently authoritative?
Who may change or act upon it?
Under what scope, purpose and conditions?
How do I know which transitions legitimately produced the present state?
How can legitimate control continue when the current implementation disappears?

Everything else can be delegated to replaceable machinery.

That gives a useful definition:

The constitutional kernel is the minimum information and rules necessary to answer those six questions without trusting any particular replaceable component to answer them merely because it possesses them.

14. Therefore, sovereignty is not primarily a security property

Security mechanisms help enforce it, but the underlying property is architectural.

Confidentiality alone is insufficient.

Integrity alone is insufficient.

Authentication alone is insufficient.

Cryptographic possession alone is insufficient.

Availability alone is insufficient.

Auditability alone is insufficient.

Decentralisation alone is insufficient.

Local storage alone is insufficient.

Self-hosting alone is insufficient.

Even perfect cryptography cannot repair an architecture whose rule is:

$$ PossessesKey \Rightarrow Sovereign $$

because then compromise or replacement of the key changes sovereignty by definition.

The deeper requirement is:

$$ Authority = GovernedInterpretation(CurrentState, Evidence, ProposedTransition) $$

Cryptographic mechanisms, credentials, hardware and providers can then serve as evidence and enforcement mechanisms beneath that rule.

15. The irreducible safety laws

The entire derivation can finally be compressed into these laws:

Continuity law

$$ Replace(Component) \not\Rightarrow Replace(Actor) $$

Authority law

$$ Possession \not\Rightarrow Authority $$

Computation law

$$ Computation \not\Rightarrow Authority $$

Proposal law

$$ Proposal \not\Rightarrow Consequence $$

Freshness law

$$ PastAuthorization \not\Rightarrow CurrentAuthorization $$

Delegation law

$$ DelegateAuthority \subseteq DelegatorAuthority $$

Disclosure law

$$ ExternalView \subseteq InformationNeededForPurpose $$

Evidence law

$$ Evidence \not\Rightarrow AuthoritativeTruth $$

Transition law

$$ AuthoritativeStateChange \Rightarrow ValidGovernedTransition $$

Recovery law

$$ Loss(ReplaceableArtifact) \not\Rightarrow Loss(ConstitutionalControl) $$

Uncertainty law

$$ UnknownOutcome \neq Success \neq Failure $$

And one meta-law sits above all of them:

$$ \boxed{ \text{No replaceable artifact may become the ultimate source of its own authority.} } $$

That is the smallest coherent foundation I can derive from your premises.

If that law and the invariants above hold, languages, models, clouds, devices, cryptographic systems, databases, runtimes and providers can all be treated as replaceable machinery.

If they do not hold, one or more of those artifacts eventually becomes the sovereign in practice.
